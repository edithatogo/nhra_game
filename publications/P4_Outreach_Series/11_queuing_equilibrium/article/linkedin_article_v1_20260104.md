# Queuing Equilibrium: Why fixing EDs is harder than just "adding beds"

Have you ever noticed that when you build a new highway lane, traffic just fills it up?
This is **Induced Demand**.

The same thing happens in Emergency Departments (EDs).
This is the **Queuing Equilibrium**.

## The real-world situation

Patients have a choice:
1.  **Go to GP**: Cost $ (gap fee), Wait (days).
2.  **Go to ED**: Cost $0, Wait (hours).

If the GP is expensive or unavailable, the ED becomes the rational choice, even if the wait is 4 hours.

## The "game" incentives

We model this as a Wardrop Equilibrium (like traffic flow).
Patients choose the path of lowest "total cost" (Money + Time).

If you improve ED efficiency (lower wait time), you lower the "cost" of attending ED.
Result? More patients choose ED over GP.
The wait time creeps back up until it reaches equilibrium again.

## The Equilibrium: "Wait Time Stability"

The wait time isn't just a measure of capacity. It's a measure of **Desirability** relative to the alternative (GP).
If GP access is broken, ED wait times *must* stay high to deter demand.

This is a tragedy. We are using "suffering" (waiting) as a rationing mechanism.

## How to fix it

You can't just fix the ED. You have to fix the **Alternative**.

- **Strengthen Primary Care**: Make GPs cheaper and faster (Urgent Care Clinics).
- **Divert demand**: Nurse-on-call, Virtual ED.
- **Co-payment?**: Controversial, but economists argue a small price signal shifts demand back to GPs (political suicide, though).

## Evidence / further reading

- Wardrop, J. (1952). *Road Research*.
- Duckett, S. (2022). *Perils of Place*.

## TL;DR

- ED demand responds to wait times.
- Improving ED efficiency can induce more demand ("If you build it, they will come").
- To fix ED crowding, you must improve the alternative (GP access).
