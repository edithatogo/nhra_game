# The “Discharge Game”: why bed block happens even when everyone hates it

Bed block is the nightmare of every hospital. Patients waiting in ambulances, EDs overflowing, wards full.

Everyone agrees it's bad. So why does it persist?

It’s often a **Coordination Failure** between Acute Care (Hospitals) and Downstream Providers (Aged Care / NDIS / Primary Care).

## The real-world situation

A patient in an acute bed is medically stable but needs support to leave (e.g., an Aged Care place or NDIS package).

- **Hospital (Acute)** wants to discharge to free up the bed.
- **Downstream (Aged/NDIS)** has limited capacity and strict intake rules.

## The “game” incentives

We model this as a Coordination Game.

- **Coordinate (Push/Pull)**: Both sides work extra hard to smooth the transition.
- **Fragment (Hold/Block)**: Each side protects its own resources.

If the Hospital pushes hard but Downstream blocks (due to rules or capacity), the Hospital wastes effort.
If Downstream creates capacity but the Hospital delays paperwork, Downstream wastes a spot.

## The Equilibrium: "Gridlock"

Often, the path of least resistance (Nash equilibrium) is for both sides to **Fragment**.
- Hospital: "They won't take them, so we'll wait."
- Downstream: "They aren't ready, so we won't hold a spot."

Result: **Gridlock**. The patient sits in a $2,000/day acute bed waiting for a $300/day aged care spot.

## How to fix it

Coordination games are solved by **trust** and **mechanisms**, not just funding.

- **Discharge Liaison Teams**: specialized roles that bridge the gap.
- **Transitional Care Funding**: paying for "step-down" beds that sit between the two systems.
- **Shared KPIs**: rewarding both sides for successful transition, not just occupancy.

## TL;DR

- Bed block is often a "Coordination Failure."
- Without trust/mechanisms, both sides retreat to "Fragment" strategies.
- Solving it requires bridging the gap, not just shouting at the hospital.
