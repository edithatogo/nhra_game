# Rubinstein Bargaining: Why time is money in health negotiations

Negotiations often look like a staring contest. Who will blink first?

In Game Theory, this is modelled as **Rubinstein Bargaining**.
The key variable isn't just "power"—it's **Patience**.

## The real-world situation

The Federal and State governments have to sign a new NHRA every 5 years.
- **If they sign quickly**: Funding flows, certainty is high.
- **If they delay**: The system drifts, uncertainty rises.

Usually, one side is more "desperate" for a deal than the other.
- If the State is running out of cash, they have a **high discount rate** (impatience).
- If the Federal government has a surplus, they can afford to wait.

## The "game" incentives

Rubinstein's model shows that the "share of the pie" you get depends on how patient you are relative to your opponent.

If I know you *need* money by Tuesday, I can offer you a bad deal on Monday, knowing you can't afford to say no.

## The Equilibrium: "Instant Agreement" (but skewed)

In a perfect information world, we don't actually wait.
I *know* you are impatient. You *know* I know.
So I make an offer that gives you *just enough* to say yes immediately.

We sign on Day 1, but the deal is heavily skewed towards the patient player.

## What this means for the NHRA

If States are always fiscally stressed, they are structurally disadvantaged in negotiations.
They accept "good enough" deals because they can't afford a stalemate.

To get a better deal, States need to lower their discount rate—build fiscal buffers so they can credibly threaten to walk away.

## Evidence / further reading

- Rubinstein, A. (1982). *Perfect Equilibrium in a Bargaining Model*.
- NHRA 2020-25 Agreement Process.

## TL;DR

- Negotiation power comes from Patience (low discount rate).
- If you need a deal fast, you will get a worse deal.
- States need fiscal buffers to negotiate effectively with the Commonwealth.
